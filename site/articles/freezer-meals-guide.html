Freezer Meals 101: How to Freeze and Reheat Food Safely

By the Hearthside Kitchen team · Updated November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Cooking a big batch and freezing the extras is one of the smartest habits in the kitchen. It saves money, cuts down on waste, and means a homemade meal is always minutes away. Freezing is simple, but a few basic rules keep your food safe and tasting its best.

Keep your freezer cold enough

According to the U.S. Department of Agriculture (USDA), food stored constantly at 0°F stays safe indefinitely, although its quality slowly declines over time. An inexpensive appliance thermometer kept in the freezer lets you check the temperature at a glance. Your refrigerator should be at 40°F or below.

Cool food quickly

Do not leave cooked food sitting out on the counter for hours to cool. The USDA recommends refrigerating or freezing leftovers within 2 hours of cooking (within 1 hour if the room is above 90°F). To cool a large pot of soup or stew faster, divide it into smaller, shallow containers before putting it in the refrigerator or freezer.

What freezes well

  • Soups and stews such as beef stew and tomato soup (add cream after thawing)
  • Casseroles and pot pies, baked or unbaked
  • Meatloaf, meatballs, and stuffed peppers
  • Cooked rice, beans, and shredded chicken
  • Baked goods such as banana bread, muffins, pancakes, and cookie dough

What does not freeze well

  • Raw salad greens, cucumbers, and other watery raw vegetables
  • Cream-based sauces and soups, which can separate (freeze them before adding the cream)
  • Cooked pasta and noodles in soup, which turn mushy
  • Fried foods, which lose their crunch
  • Hard-boiled egg whites, which turn rubbery

Package food the right way

Air is the enemy of frozen food. It causes freezer burn, those dry, grayish patches that are safe to eat but taste stale. To prevent it:

  • Use containers made for the freezer, or heavy-duty zip-top freezer bags.
  • Press out as much air as possible before sealing bags.
  • Leave about 1/2 inch of space at the top of containers for liquids, which expand as they freeze.
  • Freeze soups flat in bags on a baking sheet. Once solid, they stand upright like books and save lots of space.

Label everything

It is surprising how quickly a container of chili and a container of beef stew start to look alike. Use masking tape and a permanent marker to write the name of the dish, the date, and the number of servings. Most cooked dishes taste best when eaten within 2 to 3 months.

Thaw food safely

The USDA describes three safe ways to thaw frozen food:

  1. In the refrigerator. This is the safest method. It takes time, so plan ahead; most single portions thaw overnight.
  2. In cold water. Place the food in a leak-proof bag and submerge it in cold water, changing the water every 30 minutes. Cook it right away.
  3. In the microwave. Use the defrost setting and cook the food immediately after thawing.

Never thaw food on the kitchen counter. The outside can warm into the temperature range where bacteria multiply quickly while the center is still frozen. Many soups and stews can also be reheated directly from frozen in a covered saucepan over low heat.

Reheat to 165°F

The USDA recommends reheating leftovers until they reach 165°F, measured with a food thermometer. Soups and sauces should come to a rolling boil. When microwaving, cover the dish, stir halfway through, and let it stand for a minute or two so the heat spreads evenly.

How long do leftovers last in the refrigerator?

As a general rule, cooked leftovers keep for 3 to 4 days in the refrigerator. If you will not eat them within that time, freeze them. When in doubt, throw it out.

Food safety guidance in this article is based on publicly available recommendations from the USDA Food Safety and Inspection Service. For more detail, visit fsis.usda.gov.
