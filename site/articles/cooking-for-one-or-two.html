Cooking for One or Two: Smart Tips to Save Money and Avoid Waste

By the Hearthside Kitchen team · Updated November 13, 2026 · 6 min read

Many of us spent years cooking for a full table. When the children grow up and move out, or when you find yourself living alone, the old family recipes suddenly make far too much food. Cooking for one or two people takes a slightly different approach, but it can be just as satisfying, and often less expensive than eating out. Here are practical ways to make it easier.

Scale recipes down (or cook once, eat twice)

Most recipes can be cut in half without any trouble. On every recipe on this site, you can tap the Half button to adjust the ingredient amounts automatically. Soups, stews, casseroles, and skillet dinners scale very well. Baked goods are a little more sensitive, so use a smaller pan and start checking for doneness a few minutes early.

Sometimes it is better not to scale down at all. A full pot of soup or a whole meatloaf takes the same effort as a half batch. Cook the full recipe, enjoy it fresh, and freeze the rest in single portions for nights when you do not feel like cooking.

Shop with a plan

  • Plan three or four dinners a week, not seven. Leftovers and simple meals like eggs on toast or soup and a sandwich fill in the rest.
  • Choose recipes that share ingredients. If you buy a bunch of celery for chicken noodle soup, plan beef stew or pot pie later in the week to use the rest.
  • Use the salad bar and bulk bins. Many grocery stores let you buy just a handful of chopped vegetables, a few mushrooms, or a half cup of rice, which is perfect for small recipes.
  • Ask the butcher. Meat counters will happily wrap two chicken thighs or a half pound of ground beef instead of a family pack.

Make your freezer your best friend

A well-stocked freezer is the single most useful tool for small-household cooking. Freeze single portions of soups, stews, and casseroles in labeled containers. Bread, tortillas, shredded cheese, cooked rice, and even butter all freeze well, so you can use a little at a time without waste. Our guide to freezing and reheating meals safely covers the details.

Lean on versatile staples

A few basics make it easy to put together a quick meal for one or two:

  • Eggs: scrambled, fried, or baked into make-ahead egg muffins
  • Rotisserie chicken: one bird can become sandwiches, a salad, soup, and chicken pot pie
  • Frozen vegetables: picked at their peak, they keep for months and you can use just what you need
  • Canned beans and tomatoes: the start of countless soups and skillet meals
  • Rice, pasta, and oats: inexpensive and long-lasting

Use smaller cookware

A big Dutch oven is wonderful for a crowd, but small batches can scorch in large pans. A 2-quart saucepan, an 8-inch skillet, a quarter sheet pan, and a small 1.5-quart slow cooker make cooking for one or two more successful and easier to clean. A toaster oven is also handy; it heats quickly and uses less energy than a full-size oven for small dishes.

Give leftovers a makeover

Eating the same dish three nights in a row can get tiresome. Instead, transform leftovers into something new. Leftover meatloaf becomes a sandwich. Extra roasted chicken and vegetables become a wrap or a quick soup. Day-old cornbread becomes a crunchy topping for chili.

Make mealtime something to enjoy

When you are cooking just for yourself, it is easy to eat standing at the counter. Try setting the table, using a cloth napkin, and enjoying your meal by the window or with some music. Better still, invite a friend or neighbor to share a meal once a week. Cooking for two is often more fun than cooking for one, and good food always tastes better with company.
